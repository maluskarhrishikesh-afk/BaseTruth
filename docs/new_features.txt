Paper 1 — "The Laundering Problem in Fake Documents"
The normal way fraud detection works:
Systems look at a document and ask: "Is this fake or real?" — two options only.

The problem with that:
A fraudster can forge a document, then simply re-save it as a JPEG. That one extra save step washes away most of the digital fingerprints that scream "this was tampered with." A two-option system then calls it clean.

What this research does:
It introduces 4 categories instead of 2:

Fresh original — straight from the source
Re-saved original — still genuine, but passed through an extra save (like WhatsApp sharing does)
Directly tampered — forged, with clear evidence left behind
Tampered then re-saved — forged, but the evidence was softened by re-saving
Splitting into these 4 buckets catches the "laundering" trick. It also stops innocent re-saved genuine documents from being wrongly flagged as suspicious.

Why it's new: No previous research paper described this 4-category split, especially the idea that re-saving a fake document is an intentional evasion trick.

Performance: 93.8% accuracy on real documents, not made-up data.

Paper 2 — "Checking a Document from 11 Different Angles at Once"
The normal way:
Most research papers check documents using 1 or 2 techniques (e.g., just ELA, or just metadata).

What this research does:
It runs 11 completely different checks simultaneously on every document — each looking for a different type of forgery:

Pixel-level edits left by copy-pasting
Software tool names hidden in file metadata
Unnatural patterns left by AI-generated images
Cut-paste edges where the sharpness doesn't match
Duplicate blocks that were cloned from elsewhere
...and 6 more
For PDFs specifically, there's a separate set of 11 checks for things like hidden text, suspicious JavaScript, and signature gaps.

Why it's new: Using all 11 together, targeting financial documents specifically (payslips, bank statements), and having a system that gracefully handles both scanned photos and digital PDFs through the same output format — that combination hasn't been done before.

Paper 3 — "The Whole Pipeline as One System"
This is a "system paper" — it describes the entire BaseTruth platform end-to-end, not just one algorithm.

What makes it publishable:

A document goes in → AI figures out what type it is → the right forensics engine runs → fields are extracted → arithmetic is checked (e.g., does gross pay minus tax actually equal net pay?) → a verdict with plain-English explanation comes out
Seven industry-specific rule sets (payroll, banking, insurance, healthcare, etc.) that check domain-specific rules
A live video face verification system built directly into a browser — no app needed
Every verdict shows why it was made, not just a score
Why it's new: Most fraud tools are point solutions — one thing doing one job. This paper documents how all the pieces connect into a working, deployable system for real financial background checks.

Paper 4 — "Catching Fake Faces Without Special Hardware"
The problem:
When a company does an identity check over video (KYC — Know Your Customer), a fraudster can hold up a printed photo of someone else, or play a video from a phone screen. Systems need to detect this.

Existing approaches and their gaps:

"Move your head" challenges — can be faked if someone knows the challenge sequence ahead of time
Fancy depth cameras / infrared sensors — not available on a normal laptop or phone camera
What this research does:
It detects fake faces using a normal webcam only, through 24 different passive signals — things the camera already captures without any special setup:

IOD-Yaw trick: When a real 3D face turns sideways, the distance between the eyes shrinks (because you're seeing them at an angle). A flat printed photo's eye distance stays the same no matter how you tilt it. That difference alone is a strong signal — no depth sensor needed.
Screen grid detection: When someone films a monitor showing a face video, the screen's pixel grid creates a faint repeating pattern in the camera image. The system detects this pattern via a mathematical frequency analysis.
Hold-and-return challenge: Instead of "just turn your head once," the system requires you to hold the position for 1 second and then come back. A replay video injecting a single frame cannot fake a sustained hold.
Self-labeling: The system starts with no training data, collects real sessions as it runs, lets operators label them, and automatically trains itself when enough labels exist — so a small company can deploy it from day one without a pre-built dataset.
Privacy-first recording: Only suspicious sessions are saved as video. Normal genuine sessions are never recorded — because storing biometric video you don't need is a legal problem under GDPR.
Why it's new: The IOD-Yaw depth trick from a flat camera, the hold-and-return protocol design, the self-labeling bootstrap pipeline, and the GDPR-grounded recording policy — none of these have been described together in a single liveness paper before.

Bottom line across all 4 papers: BaseTruth's research isn't about inventing new maths — it's about identifying real-world gaps (the laundering trick, the hardware assumption, the cold-start problem) and building practical, working solutions that fill those gaps in ways nobody has documented before.
